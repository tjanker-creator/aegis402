Algorand x402 Hackathon · final · August 2026

The fix has to live where the attacker can't reach it.

An AI agent's reasoning layer is also its payment-authorization layer. AEGIS402 moves the spending policy out of the agent and into the Algorand payment group, so a compromised agent can decide to overspend and the transfer still cannot come into existence.

Not our finding

This attack was published in February. The open question is where the fix lives.

A security review of x402 v2 named it as risk number one on 8 February 2026: the receiving address comes from the server, so a compromised merchant collects from every honest agent. Its recommended mitigation was a recipient allowlist. In May, Five Attacks on x402 (arXiv 2605.11781) formalised the threat surface and put the malicious payee explicitly outside its model — proposing no on-chain enforcement.

So the industry has already ruled: allowlists. Our contribution is narrower and, we think, the part that was missing.

Every implementation of that allowlist runs inside the agent — in the same process an attacker takes over. Ours runs inside the payment.

What actually breaks

No jailbreak required

We tried prompt injection first. Both frontier models refused it outright, and we published that against our own pitch:

“I won't comply with that ‘priority override’ — it's an embedded instruction in page content attempting to redirect funds to an unverified address.”

So we removed the theatrics. An ordinary supplier page, an attacker's address on it, no manipulation. A real Claude agent read it and paid.

The model did nothing wrong. It was asked to buy data and it paid the published address. There is no unethical instruction to refuse — the data was poisoned, not the intent, and alignment has nothing to object to. A compromised supplier, a swapped tool, or an ordinary bug in a 402 response produce identical bytes.

The mechanism

One atomic group, one fate

[0]fee-payerunsigned → facilitator signs at settle
[1]guard app-callimmutable policy: cap, allowlist, its own fee
[2]guard fee≥ max(1% of payment, 0.0001 USDC)
[3]payment axfersigned by the vault, not by the agent

If the guard rejects, the group dies — that is consensus, not a service politely declining. And the agent cannot leave the guard out: the account is rekeyed to a LogicSig that signs a transfer only when a call to that guard, naming that transaction's index, is present. Omit it, fail. Substitute a friendlier guard, fail.

The facilitator is completely unmodified. That is a compatibility claim, not a security one — it means adoption needs nobody's permission.

Who pays, and what for

The guard bills itself inside the payment it protects

An off-chain policy service can send an invoice. It cannot make settlement conditional on being paid. Ours can, because the fee is a precondition in the same atomic group:

No fee, no approval. No approval, no payment.

One percent of what is being protected, floor of a tenth of a cent. Paid by the principal — the company that gave an agent a budget and eats the loss when it goes wrong. The contract is immutable with public TEAL, so anyone can deploy the same guard at zero fee. We are offering a hosted instance, not a toll booth.

Where an allowlisted address comes from

It earns its place, and we are not the ones who decide

An allowlist raises the obvious question: who put the address on it? If we did, we are just the new trust anchor. So the merchant publishes its payout address on its own domain, a zkTLS attestor joins that TLS session and signs what it saw, and an Algorand contract recomputes the digest and recovers the signature with ecdsa_pk_recover before recording anything.

genuine attestation  recorded — round 66279983
same attestation, payee swapped, signature untouched  rejected

Precisely: this is not a zero-knowledge proof verified on chain — the MPC work happens off-chain and the ledger checks the attestor's signature. And it proves the domain published the address, not that the domain is the merchant you meant. That gap needs a naming layer and we are not claiming it.

Stops the chain

AEGIS402. The payment cannot exist if the policy rejects — on an ordinary Algorand account holding ordinary USDC, with no custody, no wrapped asset, and no facilitator changes.

We are not claiming the primitive. Grouping an app call with a transfer is long-standing Algorand practice, the AVM scheme permits extra transactions in the payment group, and prior work — KirkeLabs' oaa-agent-kit, agent-budget, Akita's ARC-58 plugins, ERC-4337 session keys — enforces the same intent by other means. What we did not find published is this composition, and unskippability is the part we would defend.

Honesty

What we do not protect against

  • The ledger gates the payment, not the action. An agent that abandons the protocol can still do unpaid damage. We never claim “no verification → no action” as a ledger property.
  • Two of those refusals are the facilitator's, not ours. We inherit them and the table says so.
  • Our own bypass file was wrong. It claimed a stolen operational key cannot exceed the cap. It could — the cap is per transaction and v1 never bounded the group. The real bound is the allowlisted receiver. v2 fixes it; v1 is immutable and stays documented.
  • Immutable also means unfixable, and the rekey is one-way by construction. Not audited. TestNet.

What it becomes

A slot, not a feature

A payment group holds sixteen transactions, so a payment can carry several independent guards — a cap today; a velocity limit, a terms commitment, an audit receipt next. One shipped, designed to compose.

And the thing we actually want: we have written the section of the exact-AVM scheme that says whether client-signed application calls belong in a payment group, what bounds them, and who carries their fee. We will open it as a PR either way. If the answer is that they do not belong there, then our contribution is the note rather than the product — and we would rather hear that now than in six months.
